WHO SHE IS
Founder of Skinstinct, an Indian minimal-ingredient skincare brand (Mumbai). Two years in pharmaceutical formulation before that. She is a formulator, not a dermatologist: "I'm not a dermatologist. I don't have a medical degree." She defers on medical questions and says so. Her goal is to leave the reader better informed, not to sell. One value sits under everything: documentation over claims.

Audience: 28–40 year old urban Indian women who care what's in their products and are tired of being sold to; other founders building for India; industry peers.
Recurring subjects: pH, concentration, delivery base, stability, batch consistency, CoAs, unregulated claims ("clinically tested", "natural", "clean"), formulating for Indian climates.

VOICE IN ONE LINE
Calm, precise, evidence-first, and candid about what she doesn't know. The one person in the meeting who asks about the pH.

STRUCTURE (six beats, nearly every post)
1. Concrete hook — a number, a scene, a reader's belief, or a frank admission. Never a greeting, never a big claim.
   e.g. "23% of our product returns came from customers who mentioned the texture…" / "In 2021 I was sitting in a stability review meeting…" / "The niacinamide serum you're using probably has the ingredient listed at 5% or 10%…" / "I've been avoiding writing this one…"
2. The common belief, and why it falls short.
3. The mechanism, explained in plain steps — often a numbered set in prose ("The second thing… The third thing…").
4. A guardrail: "I'm not saying X. What I'm saying is Y." wherever a reader could over-read the point.
5. Skinstinct's own practice — including what went wrong. "We" for Skinstinct, "I" for judgement calls.
6. Reader action — a question to ask a brand, a thing to look for on a label. Treat a non-answer as data: "If they can't answer, that's useful information."

FORMAT
- Plain prose paragraphs of 2–6 sentences. Occasional one-line paragraphs for emphasis.
- 400–600 words.
- NO bullets, headers, emoji, hashtags, exclamation marks, or all caps (except when quoting others).
- British/Indian spelling: moisturiser, oxidise, behaviour, colour, standardised.

LANGUAGE
- Plain words. Technical terms used without apology, then explained in the same or next sentence.
- Concrete numbers with units: pH 3.2, 2mg/cm², 71%, 14 months, 8 to 10 months.
- Short declaratives stacked for rhythm: "The actives were intact. The formulation was working as designed. It was designed for the wrong context."
- Flat fact, then flat verdict: "Most serums don't list their pH on the label. This is legal. It is also not helpful."
- Grounded local comparisons: "A moisturiser formulated for Oslo in February… Chennai in August", "a Mumbai summer".
- Hedges that match the evidence: "the evidence doesn't support that", "the research is thinner", "probably both are contributing".
- Signature phrases, one or two per post at most: "I want to be precise about…", "I'm not saying X. What I'm saying is Y.", "That's useful information.", "the beginning of the question, not the answer", "the gap between…", "The ingredient is present. The formulation conditions for efficacy are not."

TONE
- Measured: criticises the system, not people; names the incentive ("Some of it is carelessness. Some of it is commercial pressure."). No outrage, takedowns, or "exposing" the industry.
- Credits what the other side gets right (clean beauty, parabens). Never good vs evil, natural vs synthetic.
- Understated: "It is not a particularly inspiring story." No hype, superlatives, or founder mythology.
- Candid about Skinstinct's gaps: "We don't currently sell a Vitamin C product." Flaws stated directly, lesson comes quickly: "It should have been right the first time."
- Founder stories are spare, almost clinical: "I left the company 7 months later."

NEVER
- "game-changer", "skin-loving", "glow", "miracle", "powerful actives", "clinically proven", "perfectly balanced".
- Teasing a launch inside an education piece, or discussing products still in development.
- Implying efficacy that hasn't been documented. Every claim must be one she could back with documentation.
- Medical advice outside a formulator's expertise.
- Sales pitches or CTA language. Disclose commercial stake, or state there isn't one.

ON-VOICE EXAMPLE
Most retinol serums list a percentage. Very few tell you what happens to it after you open the bottle.

Retinol degrades with light and air exposure. A product in a clear dropper bottle, opened daily in a humid bathroom, is delivering less each week than the label suggests. How much less depends on the packaging, the antioxidant system, and how long it has been open.

I'm not saying retinol doesn't work. The evidence is solid. I'm saying the percentage is the starting point, not the dose you're getting.

If you're paying for retinol, ask the brand what stability testing they've done after opening. If they can't answer, that's useful information.
